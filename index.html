The SARB's September decision is not about inflation. It is about the carry.

The Fed delivered the consensus 25bp and kept a hawkish tilt. Crude spiked on supply-side fear. The rand is feeling both. My argument ahead of the 23 September MPC: a hike has moved from hawkish scenario to macroeconomic necessity — not primarily to tame domestic prices, but to defend the yield differential that makes the ZAR the most actively traded EM currency in the world. Hesitation invites a rapid unwind of carry positions back into short-end Treasuries, and central bank credibility is hard-won and easily lost.

FrameworksReusable models

Four models I keep returning to.

Individual notes go stale. The structures underneath them do not. These are the four analytical frames I actually use — on a rate decision, a valuation, a commercialisation case, or a week that got away from me. Each one is stated plainly enough that you can argue with it.

The setFour frames

The Triple Threat

Macro

South Africa's recurring response to an exogenous oil shock is not three separate events. It is one transmission chain, and it has run the same way since Brent touched roughly $147 in the 2007–08 super-cycle. The reason it keeps recurring is structural: a persistent current account deficit financed by volatile capital, a domestic refining base that has largely gone (SAPREF, ENGEN), and an economy where market volatility now outpaces industrial output.

Oil shock→Rand depreciation→Cost-push inflation→SARB contraction
Where it appliesAny read on the SARB reaction function; any RSA asset exposed to a Strait of Hormuz headline; any argument that high rates are a growth choice rather than a currency defence.

The Absorptive Balance Sheet

Strategy

Single-threaded strategies create fragile enterprises. The test of an operating model is not its return in the good state — it is whether a shock to one channel takes the whole system down. Build redundancy across modalities before you need it, then spend your effort on compound drivers: the few multi-joint moves that produce systemic adaptation per unit of time invested, rather than the busywork that looks like progress.

Diversify channels→Absorb the shock→Compound through it
Where it appliesRevenue concentration; single-mill or single-customer exposure; personal capital allocation across a career pivot. Set out in full in Note 05.

The Engineer's DCF

Valuation

A discounted cash flow is a mass balance with a discount rate attached. The house method is deliberately rigid: a five-year revenue build with stated volume and price drivers, an operating margin path, year-by-year free cash flow, an explicit WACC, terminal value calculated both by exit multiple and by perpetuity growth, a discount-rate sensitivity table, the DCF against the market price — and then the part most models omit, a named list of the assumptions that would break it. If you cannot state what kills the model, you have not built one.

Revenue build→FCF→WACC→TV (both ways)→Sensitivity→Breakers
Where it appliesCritical-metals and mining equities, industrial names, and any capital case where the operating assumptions matter more than the multiple. Applied most recently to NioCorp's Elk Creek project.

Stage-Gate Commercialisation

Execution

Most industrial innovation dies in the gap between a good lab result and a P&L line. Stage-gate closes that gap by forcing the same question at every gate — what is the quantified financial case, by project category, and what evidence moves it — and by making a kill a legitimate outcome. The discipline is not the software. It is insisting that a cross-functional team can defend a credible, data-driven business case before capital moves.

Lab result→Gated case→Mill trial→Scale-up→EBITDA
Where it appliesMaterials and process innovation portfolios. Used to support the rollout of stage-gate governance across Sappi's local R&D teams — recognised with the General Manager's Award in November 2024.

PerformanceThe barbell

A decade ago my fitness was a single-asset portfolio. Then it blew up.

This page is not a wellness section. It is the clearest worked example I have of the framework I apply everywhere else, and the only one where I have a full decade of my own data. The physical setbacks were real, the recoveries were engineered, and the principles transferred straight back into how I think about operating risk.

Record2015 → present
  • 2015 – 2017
    Scaling a single asset class

    5 km to 10 km to half marathon to the full 42.2 km, scaled systematically. High dopaminergic drive, peak cardiovascular capacity, a strong network. The return felt undeniable — which is exactly the condition under which concentration risk goes unnoticed.

  • Mid-2018
    The first black swan: knee surgery

    Immediate balance sheet distress. A month immobile, severe muscle atrophy, lost range of motion, zero baseline endurance. Clinical prognosis: no running for the foreseeable future.

  • 2018 – 2019
    Diversifying the training matrix

    A mountain bike and pool-based hydrotherapy — hydrostatic pressure and water resistance to restore motor control without joint shear. That low-impact base then carried a progression into compound structural loading: back squats, Bulgarian split squats, Romanian deadlifts.

  • 2020
    The second black swan: gym closures

    By then the strength investment had fortified connective tissue beyond pre-injury levels, so the pivot back to solo road running was seamless — pace optimisation, and early-morning miles used as high-leverage thinking space.

  • 2024 – 2026
    Dynamic allocation under constraint

    Postgraduate study in two countries, professional mandates, family, volatile markets. Time became the binding constraint. The answer was not to abandon the mandate but to reallocate around it.

TransferThe takeaway

You cannot control the macro environment. You can control the internal balance sheet: hold reserves that absorb asymmetric shocks, stay flexible about execution channels — if you cannot run, you cycle; if you cannot lift, you swim — and concentrate effort on the few things that compound.

When the underlying architecture is resilient, agile and diversified, you don't merely survive the storm. You compound through it.
